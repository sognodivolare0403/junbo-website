// 01 — ABOUT

THE LENS AND THE FIST

I have dedicated over a decade to martial arts, chiefly competitive Wushu. What began as physical discipline became a philosophical lens: De — virtue. To be like water, to stay centered, to align my action with what I know, and to seek perspectives unlike my own.

At sixteen, I picked up a camera to document the world I knew best: training halls, masters, and tight-knit communities formed through shared practice. My documentary work has moved from Wenzhou to Nanjing, from school corridors to public squares.

I am pursuing Journalism and Documentary Studies through the methods of visual anthropology: grounded observation, careful listening, and cinematic storytelling.

// 05 — INDEPENDENT RESEARCH

THE MAKING OF KUNG FU

Applies the theory of kitsch and the “second tear” to examine how Fist of Fury transforms individual emotion into a collective imagination of national victory. Through close readings of the “Sick Man of East Asia” sign, Chen Zhen’s attack on the Japanese dojo, Bruce Lee’s physical performance, and the film’s final sacrifice, the project asks how an individual body becomes a symbolic national body.

It argues that the film converts colonial humiliation into an emotionally powerful narrative of resistance, while also questioning what this unity conceals: colonial Hong Kong, the commercialization of nationalist feeling, the real-world limits of martial arts, and the instability of a singular Chinese identity.

PHASE 1

Conceptual Framework

Defining kitsch, the first tear, and the second tear: how an individual response becomes imagined collective feeling.

PHASE 2

Film & Body Analysis

Close reading the sign, the dojo sequence, Bruce Lee’s physical performance, and Chen Zhen’s final sacrifice.

PHASE 3

Historical & Cultural Critique

Colonial Hong Kong, commercial cinema, nationalist emotion, martial-arts mythology, and the contradictions behind collective victory.

RESEARCH QUESTION

“How does Fist of Fury transform Bruce Lee’s body into a symbolic national body—and what does the ‘second tear’ reveal about the emotional construction of Chinese collective identity?”

THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

◆ Kundera’s Kitsch — The first tear responds to heroic victory; the second responds to the belief that an entire community is moved by the same image.
◆ The National Body — Bruce Lee’s muscles, anger, speed, and sacrifice become collective proof that the Chinese body is no longer weak or humiliated.
◆ Colonial & Commercial Cinema — The film creates an emotionally unified China while emerging from colonial Hong Kong and selling resistance as cinematic pleasure.
